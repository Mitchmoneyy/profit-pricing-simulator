How LTV and CAC are calculated

Contribution per sale = price × (1 − refund rate) − delivery cost − (price × fee% + $0.30)
LTV (one-time) = contribution × orders per customer
LTV (subscription) = contribution × 1 ÷ monthly churn, capped at 60 months
CAC = monthly ad spend ÷ new customers per month
LTV:CAC = LTV ÷ CAC

LTV here is based on contribution, not revenue. Revenue-based LTV flatters the ratio because it ignores what each sale costs you to deliver.

What is a good LTV:CAC ratio?

RatioWhat it means
Below 1×Each customer loses money over their lifetime. Growth makes it worse.
1–3×Profitable on paper, with little room for rising ad costs or refunds.
3–5×The usual target. Acquisition pays for itself with margin to spare.
Above 5×Often a sign you could spend more to grow faster.

Worked example

A subscription at $79 a month with $9 of delivery cost, 2.9% + $0.30 fees and 2% refunds contributes about $66 a month. At 4% monthly churn, customers stay 25 months on average, so LTV is about $1,646. If $16,000 of ads wins 540 customers, CAC is $29.63 and LTV:CAC is about 56×, a strong signal to spend more on acquisition.

Frequently asked questions

Should LTV use revenue or profit?

Profit, specifically contribution after refunds, delivery and fees. Using revenue overstates lifetime value, sometimes by several times for physical products.

Why cap subscription lifetime at 60 months?

Very low churn gives unrealistic lifetimes (0.5% churn implies 16 years). Capping at five years keeps the estimate conservative.

What is payback period?

How many orders or months of contribution it takes to earn back what you spent to acquire the customer. Shorter payback means growth needs less cash.

How do I improve LTV:CAC?

Raise contribution per sale (price, delivery cost, refunds), increase repeat orders or reduce churn, or lower CAC by improving conversion on the traffic you already pay for.
